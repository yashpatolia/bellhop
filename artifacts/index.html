Bellhop: the robot that answers the call bell

A small AI robot that rolls to a resident's room when the bell rings, talks to them, and calls a nurse only when the situation needs one.

Cal Hacks 13 · Team: Yash, Aditya, Gordon, Jeremy

Project planAgents, hardware, build plan, demo script, cost model ResearchNumbers, sponsors, Jev, robots, sources Extra ideasFeature backlog with hours and status

The idea

Care homes are short-staffed, and one nurse can be responsible for 20 to 50 residents. A nurse cannot tell from the bell alone whether a ring is a fall or a request for a cup of tea, so every ring pulls them away from someone who may need clinical care.

Pitch: don't send a nurse to every bell. Send a robot first, and let it work out whether a nurse is needed.

52%

of hospital staff said calls actually needed a nurse, in a survey of 808 staff.

9 minutes

average response in one 300-bed nursing home; about half of calls missed its 5-minute standard.

3.68 vs 4.10

nursing hours per resident per day in US homes, against the 4.10 recommended.

What we claim: less nurse time on non-urgent calls and faster responses, both measurable on the dashboard. We do not claim lives saved, and Bellhop never diagnoses. Sources and caveats are on the Research page.

How it works

Bellhop reads the resident's memory before it speaks, so it already knows who it is talking to. Urgency 1 and 2 never reach a nurse, 3 arrives with a short written summary, and 4 sets off an alert immediately while the robot stays with the resident.

Features

Core

Robot

Bell-to-bedside navigation

Rolls to the room by following a floor line and reading a QR code per door, stops for obstacles, parks facing the resident.

Voice

Companion conversation

Streaming speech in and out with an energetic, kid-like voice. Stays calm and gentle with confused residents.

Triage

Urgency score 1 to 4

Jev gives a fast score with probabilities; Claude writes the plain-English summary. Leans toward escalation when unsure.

Memory

Resident memory

Likes, family, routines and mood are recalled in milliseconds, so the robot greets people by name and builds on the last visit.

Staff

Live call feed, robot status, transcripts, alerts, and a nurse-minutes-saved counter with the assumption shown on screen.

Staff

Pings the right person with a short summary, or creates a task for an aide, the kitchen or family.

Staff

Dashboard metrics

Robot

Status lights

LED colours for idle, listening, nurse on the way and emergency, readable across a room.

Why a robot and not cameras or other alternatives

Most alternatives either watch people or add alerts. Bellhop is the only one that responds to the person who rang, which is the actual bottleneck.

AlternativeWhat it doesWhere it falls short
Cameras in roomsWatches for falls and unusual activityConstant surveillance of private spaces raises consent and dignity problems. It sees but cannot answer, comfort, or tell a nurse why someone rang. Needs someone watching feeds or an alert queue
Wearable pendants and fall sensorsDetect a fall or let a resident press a buttonResidents forget or refuse to wear them. They detect events, not the many non-emergency calls
Floor or bed sensorsDetect leaving bed or a fallMany false alarms, and no way to talk to the resident
Hire more staffDirectly adds handsCostly and often impossible; the staffing shortage is the problem we are working around
Smart speakers or voice assistantsAnswer questions and set remindersFixed in one spot, no sense of urgency, and not connected to nurses
Companion robots (e.g. ElliQ)Tabletop AI companionStationary and not tied to the call bell; they chat but do not decide who should respond
Delivery robots (e.g. Moxi)Fetch and carry suppliesDo not talk to residents or triage calls
Our answer: the bell already means "I need something". Bellhop goes to that person, listens, and filters the call, with no always-on camera and no new device for the resident to wear. Privacy is a feature: it only listens during a visit, and the camera is used on demand, which is a roadmap point to discuss with care homes. The claim about other robots comes from a quick search, so check it before calling this novel on stage.

Reminiscence mode

A scheduled, gentle conversation about the resident's own past. It is useful for lonely or low-mood residents and, with care, for people with memory loss, where recalling familiar things is often calming. It is a companionship activity, not therapy, and we do not make clinical claims.

Builds on memory

Uses the resident's profile: hometown, job, family, favourite music, photos staff or family have added. Each visit adds new details, so the next one goes deeper.

Era-aware prompts

Knows roughly when they were young and brings up music, films, news and everyday life from those years ("what did you do on a Saturday night in 1965?").

Photo and music prompts

Shows a photo on the touchscreen or plays a song from their era and asks what it reminds them of.

Life story capture

Turns the stories they tell into short notes on the profile, which staff and, with consent, family can read. It also helps new staff get to know the resident.

Mood signal

Engagement, laughter and tone are logged, so the mood trend shows which topics work and flags when someone is quieter than usual.

Safe by design

If a topic upsets them, the robot changes subject gently and logs it. It never corrects a confused resident, and it alerts staff if distress grows.

Learning from every call

Every visit is a data point. Keeping the data structured is cheap now and is what makes Bellhop improve over time and prove its value.

  • Call reasons: category, urgency, whether the robot resolved it, time to first response, and whether a nurse later disagreed with the triage.
  • Resident feedback: a quick spoken rating after a visit, plus tone and engagement, per resident and over time.
  • Patterns for staff: calls by room, hour and category (for example "most calls at 3 pm are toileting"), which helps schedule rounds before residents ring.
  • Triage tuning: every nurse override becomes a labelled example for setting thresholds.

Scaling to a fleet

The demo uses one robot, but the real goal is a fleet covering a whole care home and then many homes. The design already supports that: agents are small and independent, and shared state lives in one realtime store.

Smart dispatch

The Dispatcher picks the nearest free robot by position, battery and workload, or sends a nurse as well when risk is high. Our multi-robot simulation on the dashboard shows this.

Charging and uptime

Robots dock to charge, report battery and faults, and hand calls to another robot when low.

Multi-site dashboard

Each home sees only its own residents, while an operator view compares robots, homes, response times and cost per resident.

Integrates with existing systems

Reads real call-bell signals and care-plan notes through adapters instead of replacing them. This is on the roadmap, not in the demo.

Shared learning, private data

Triage thresholds and prompts improve across homes from anonymised outcomes, while resident data stays per site.

Costs scale linearly

Hardware is one-time and API fees are per use, so there are no per-seat licences. The cost model on the Project page covers fleets up to 50 robots.

Scaling raises real questions we have not solved: doors and elevators, infection control, wifi coverage, regulation and liability. We say so rather than hide it.

Demo

Three short scenes, one per outcome tier, with the dashboard on screen throughout: a lonely resident asking for tea (urgency 1, no nurse alert), an aching hip (urgency 3, summary and "on my way"), and a fall (urgency 4, red LEDs and an immediate alert). The closing shot is the minutes-saved counter.

Alternative opening: judges play the residents. Bellhop introduces itself, a teammate holds prompt cards like "ASK IT TO PLAY A GAME", and after about a minute we switch to the dashboard to show the conversation recorded and the judge now in the database as a resident. The venue will be loud, so the judge speaks close to the robot.

Guardrails: prototype only with teammates as residents and no real patient data. The bell stays a bell. When unsure, treat the call as more urgent. No diagnosis. Gentle and never argumentative with confused residents.
